Book of Enoch

The Book of Enoch (also called 1 Enoch) is an ancient Jewish apocalyptic text traditionally ascribed to Enoch, the great-grandfather of Noah. Most scholars date the older sections (especially the Book of the Watchers) to approximately 300–200 BC, while the Book of Parables is generally placed around the 1st century BC. It is not included in the Jewish or most Christian canons, but it is regarded as scripture by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church and the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church.

The book expands on Genesis 6 and contains unique material about the fall of the Watchers (angels), the origin of evil spirits and the Nephilim, the reasons for the Flood, heavenly journeys, astronomical secrets, dream visions, and the coming judgment and messianic kingdom.

Chapters 1–36. This is the oldest major section. It opens with Enoch’s blessing and a vision of the coming judgment. The core narrative describes the fall of the Watchers (200 angels led by Semjaza and Azazel) who descend to earth, take human wives, and teach forbidden knowledge (weapon-making, cosmetics, astrology, etc.). Their offspring, the giants (Nephilim), bring violence and corruption. The archangels report the chaos to God, who decrees judgment: the Flood will cleanse the earth, the Watchers will be bound, and the spirits of the giants will become evil spirits that afflict humanity. Enoch is taken on journeys through the cosmos and shown the places of punishment and blessing.

Chapters 37–71. This section contains three parables (similitudes) focused on the coming judgment, the Son of Man / Elect One, and the vindication of the righteous. It develops a strong messianic figure who is pre-existent, sits on the throne of glory, judges the kings and the mighty, and dwells with the righteous. Themes include the resurrection, the transformation of heaven and earth, and the final defeat of evil. Many scholars see close parallels with later New Testament “Son of Man” language.

Chapters 72–82. Also called the Book of the Heavenly Luminaries. The angel Uriel reveals to Enoch the laws of the sun, moon, stars, and calendar. It strongly defends a 364-day solar calendar and criticizes those who follow a lunar calendar. Detailed descriptions of the gates of heaven, the winds, the seasons, and the movement of the luminaries are given. The section ends with Enoch’s predictions about the moral decline of later generations.

Chapters 83–90. Contains two dream visions. The first is a short vision of the Flood. The second is the famous “Animal Apocalypse,” an allegorical history of the world from Adam to the Maccabean period (and beyond) in which humans are represented as animals and angels as stars or men. It culminates in the judgment of the fallen stars, the building of a new house (temple), and the appearance of a white bull (messianic figure) who transforms all the animals.

Chapters 91–108. This final section includes the Apocalypse of Weeks (a schematic history divided into ten “weeks”), exhortations to the righteous, woes against sinners, teachings on the two ways, and additional material about the birth of Noah and future judgment. It emphasizes moral instruction, the certainty of divine justice, and the ultimate blessing of the righteous.
